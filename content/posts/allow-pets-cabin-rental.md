I allow pets in my rentals. I have from my very first listing, so I've never run a cabin without them.

Most hosts I talk to are nervous about it, and I get why. Dogs scratch floors, shed on furniture, and now and then pee on a rug. But in my experience, the fear is bigger than the problem, and a pet policy that's set up well keeps most of the risk out.

Here's how I handle pets: what I charge, how many dogs I allow, what the wear and tear really looks like, and what I'd tell guests up front if you're in a rural spot like Hocking Hills.

---

## Why I Allow Pets in My Vacation Rentals

The simple reason is exposure. Allowing pets puts your cabin in front of a lot more people. When I started offering pet stays, only about 25% of listings allowed them. Hosts are slowly coming around, but the ones who accept pets are still the vast minority.

That matters when a traveler with a dog is searching. They have far fewer options, so your cabin gets a look that it might not get otherwise.

The other reason is the guests themselves. I've had repeat guests who come back with the same dog, and I get comments about how much fun the dog had. People who bring a dog on vacation are often bringing family, and they remember the places that welcomed everybody. If you want more on turning one-time guests into regulars, I wrote about it in [how to turn one-time guests into repeat bookers](/blog/repeat-guests).

---

## How I Set a Pet Fee (and Why It's Close to Boarding)

I price my pet fee close to what it would cost to board the dog. The logic is simple. If someone has a dog they don't want to deal with on vacation, they'll board it and leave it home. If they're willing to pay about what boarding costs to bring the dog along, they actually want the dog there.

That filters your guests. You end up with people who bring dogs that are well behaved, because those are the owners who want their dogs with them.

Right now I charge **$25 per night per pet**. Boarding runs about $35-40 a night. I used to charge $30, but competition pushed me down. $25 is the lowest I would go. Below that, I don't think the fee covers the extra work, and it stops screening anyone out.

I'd rather be honest that the number moves with the market. If other cabins near you charge less, you'll feel it in your bookings. If you're working out how a pet fee fits with the rest of your rates, my [rural cabin pricing guide](/blog/rural-cabin-pricing-guide) covers the bigger picture.

## My Pet Rules: How Many Dogs I Allow

I generally allow up to two pets per booking. That's the baseline.

I make exceptions case by case. If someone is bringing three or four small dogs, for example, I'll look at it and decide. If it's a long booking, I'm also more lenient, because I want to land that booking.

I don't think a rigid rule works for every request. A flat "two dogs, no exceptions" policy would cost me some good bookings. The deposit and the fee give me a cushion, and I use my own judgment on the rest.

---

## What Pets Actually Do to a Cabin

You do get some extra wear and tear. Here's what it looks like in my cabins:

- **Scratches in the floors** that build up over time.
- **More hair on the furniture.**
- **Bedding that needs more washing** to get the hair out.
- **Chewed furniture ends or stair treads**, now and then.
- **Pee on a rug or carpet**, again now and then.

I've never had a major issue. And I'll say this plainly: I've had more damage done by drunk humans than by dogs.

Before you commit, talk to your cleaners. I made sure mine knew pets were allowed and that they were fine with what I pay. I don't pay extra for pet turns, but some cleaners may ask for a little more per turn when pets are involved. Better to have that conversation early than find out when a turnover goes badly.

---

## Use a Damage Deposit to Keep Bad Dogs Out

I take a large damage deposit on all bookings, pets or not. Guests know up front that damage is a possibility, and that keeps the worst dogs from showing up.

The deposit and the fee work together. The fee, set close to boarding, discourages people who'd rather not bring the dog. The deposit discourages people whose dog may be a problem. The worst-behaved dogs tend to stay home.

I'm not an insurance or legal expert, so check with your insurer about how pet damage is covered, and check with your county about any local rules that apply to your rental.

---

## Rural Details to Tell Pet Guests Up Front

A rural property has a few things to spell out in your listing and your pre-arrival message:

- **Busy roads.** If the cabin is near a busy road, say so clearly. A dog that gets loose near traffic is a bad day for everyone.
- **Other cabins on the property.** If other guests, and possibly their pets, are staying close by, tell people. Nobody wants a surprise dog meeting.

Being clear about this up front is cheaper than handling a complaint afterward. It also helps with reviews, which I cover in [the rural host's guide to handling bad reviews](/blog/handling-bad-reviews). And it fits with the broader list of [things guests expect at a rural cabin](/blog/rural-cabin-guest-expectations).

## What Goes Wrong With Pets (It Happens)

Pet problems do happen. A guest brings a dog they didn't mention, or an extra one. It's hard to control sometimes, and I won't pretend my system catches everything.

My view is that you take the good with the bad. The extra exposure, the repeat guests, and the happy reviews outweigh the occasional surprise, at least in my cabins.

---

## So, Should You Allow Pets in Your Cabin Rental?

I do, and I'd do it again. Set a fee that makes sense next to what boarding costs where you are. Take a real damage deposit. Tell your cleaners. Be upfront about the road and the neighbors. Then decide on the number of dogs case by case.

You'll see more scratches and more hair. You'll also reach guests that most hosts around you are turning away, and some of them will keep coming back with their dog. That's been a good trade for me. If you're still unsure, talk through the numbers for your own property before deciding.
