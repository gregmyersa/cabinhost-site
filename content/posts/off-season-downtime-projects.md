The slow season feels like dead time if you let it. Fewer bookings, quieter calendar, less money coming in. But it's actually the best window you have all year to work on the property without displacing a single guest.

I've come to think of the off-season as project season. Here's how I think about using that downtime, from the small stuff that takes an afternoon to the bigger investments that reshape a property.

---

## Three Tiers of Off-Season Projects

Not every project needs the same level of commitment, and it helps to think in tiers.

**Small fixes and touch-ups.** Recaulking a bathtub. Fixing a loose towel rod. Touching up scuffed paint. Tightening cabinet hardware. None of these are exciting, but they're the difference between a listing that feels cared for and one that feels tired. These are the projects to knock out first — they're fast, cheap, and they compound. A guest who notices ten small things wrong will remember the cabin as run-down, even if nothing was actually broken.

**Medium projects.** Restaining a deck or the exterior of a log cabin. Repainting a room. Upgrading a fire pit. These take more time and more budget than the small stuff, but they're still manageable in a single off-season window, and they tend to have real visual impact — the kind that shows up well in photos and gets noticed in reviews.

A good fire pit deserves special mention here. It's one of the best medium-sized investments you can make, and it's on my list at multiple properties right now. A well-built fire pit area — real stone or a quality metal ring, comfortable seating around it, good lighting — photographs beautifully, and fire pit photos consistently perform well in a listing gallery. It's the kind of amenity that shows up in search filters, gets mentioned in reviews, and creates the exact image a guest is picturing when they search for a cabin getaway in the first place.

On the smaller end of the spectrum, don't overlook something like tetherball. It's just a pole and a ball, so the cost is minimal, but it punches way above its weight. It works for a two-person cabin just as well as a ten-person one, it photographs genuinely well — a simple, colorful pole against a yard or tree line is an easy, appealing shot — and it carries a kind of nostalgia that appeals to a wide range of guests, from parents remembering it from their own childhood to kids discovering it for the first time. Small footprint, small cost, real appeal.

**Bigger investments.** Finishing a basement, adding a bathroom, building out a dedicated game room, adding a swing set or playground. These are the projects that meaningfully change what your listing can offer and who it appeals to. They take more planning, more capital, and often more time — which is exactly why the off-season is the right window for them. You're not fighting around a booking calendar.

---

## What I've Actually Done With This Time

Over the last several winters, I've used the slow season to finish a basement rec room at one property, and add nice flooring and trim to another rec room, eventually turning it into a full game room.

Both of those investments paid off in ways I could actually measure — higher nightly rates I was able to charge, more bookings, and guests specifically calling out the space in their reviews. That combination — rate, occupancy, and reviews all moving in the right direction from one project — is about as clear a signal as you'll get that an investment was worth it.

---

## What I'm Planning Next

Right now I have a running list across my properties. At one cabin, I want to add a half bath in the downstairs rec room. At another, I'm looking at a swing set or playground and an upgraded fire pit. At a third, it's an upgraded fire pit and restaining the deck.

But I'm also being honest with myself about where I am in the cycle. The last few years went heavily into big projects — the basement finish, the game room build-out, adding hot tubs to a couple of properties, and absorbing two new cabins into the portfolio, one of which needed serious investment in drainage work and another that came with a backlog of deferred maintenance.

So the next few years are going to lean smaller. More touch-ups, more medium projects, more catching up on the deferred maintenance that piled up while I was focused on bigger builds. That's a normal part of the cycle — you can't sprint on major renovations indefinitely, and a season focused on tightening up the small stuff is just as valuable as a season spent on a big build.

---

## How to Decide What to Tackle

My approach comes down to three things: how much time I actually have that season, what my budget looks like, and what I think the return on the investment will be.

Budget is closely tied to labor. If you're doing the work yourself, your costs are largely materials and your own time — which means the bar for "worth it" is a lot lower, since you're adding long-term value to an asset without paying someone else's hourly rate. If you're hiring everything out, the math changes. Labor costs eat into the return, so the project needs a clearer case for itself before it makes sense.

That's not a reason to avoid hiring things out — some projects genuinely require it — but it does mean the calculus shifts, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which situation you're actually in before committing to a bigger build.

---

## Make It Count for the Listing

Whatever tier of project you're working on, keep one thing in mind: does this photograph well, and is it something a guest would actually search for?

A recaulked tub won't move the needle on bookings, but it needs to happen anyway — it's baseline upkeep. A new game room, an upgraded fire pit, an added bathroom — those are the projects that give you something new to photograph, something new to add as a searchable amenity on Airbnb or VRBO, and something a guest can point to as a reason to choose your property over a similar one down the road.

When you're planning your off-season project list, it's worth sorting by that lens: which of these are just necessary maintenance, and which of these actually make the listing more competitive? Both matter, but knowing which bucket a project falls into helps you budget your limited off-season time and money more intentionally.

---

## The Real Cost of Doing Nothing

There's one more thing worth saying plainly: the worst option is sitting on your hands and doing nothing.

I've done this myself in past off-seasons, and it doesn't actually save money — it just delays the cost and makes it bigger. Small deferred maintenance piles up quietly. A minor issue that would have taken an afternoon and a modest budget to fix turns into two or three compounding problems by the time you finally get to it. And once that backlog gets big enough, it crowds out the value-add improvements you actually want to be making, because now all your time and budget is going toward catching up instead of moving forward.

It's better for cash flow, and for the property, to do something every off-season — even if it feels small. A modest, steady pace of maintenance and improvement is far cheaper over time than letting things pile up and then trying to tackle it all at once. Budget for whatever you can each season, no matter how minor it seems, and do it. The alternative almost always costs more later.

---

## The Off-Season Is an Asset, Not a Problem

It's easy to see the slow months only as lost revenue. But they're also the only real stretch of the year where you can work on the property without guests in the way. Used well, that time turns into higher rates, better reviews, and a property that keeps improving instead of just aging.
