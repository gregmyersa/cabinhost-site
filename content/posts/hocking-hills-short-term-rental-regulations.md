Hocking Hills has been an easy place to run a cabin for a long time. Very few rules, growing demand, and not much standing between an owner and a booking. That is changing.

As the market has grown, so has the pressure on the county to do something about it. Several short-term rental regulations are being proposed, and I think some of them may be an overreaction.

I've operated rentals here since 2015, so I have a stake in this. In this post I'll cover why the pressure is building, the one proposal that worries me most, what I think "balance" looks like, and what I'd tell any host in a market that's growing fast. This is my opinion as an operator, not legal advice. For anything that affects your property, check with your county and an attorney.

---

## Why Hocking Hills Grew So Fast

Hocking Hills was very under-regulated, and then it got national attention during the pandemic. People wanted things to do that didn't involve getting on a plane or standing in indoor crowds.

It's also a beautiful place, and much of the region can reach it without driving through the mountains. On top of that, more people wanted to get outdoors. All of that pushed demand up.

Growth like this tends to follow demand. Once a place is known, owners convert existing properties to short-term rentals, and people build new cabins specifically to rent out.

---

## The Number of Rentals Nearly Doubled in About Three Years

The figures I've seen come from AirDNA. For the Hocking Hills region, the number of rentals went from about 1,200 to about 2,200 in roughly the last three years. This is based on data from the Hocking Hills market in AirDNA, which covers most of Hocking County and a little beyond.

The market is showing signs of saturation, and things are sitting on the market longer than they did. I've felt that at my own cabins. Rates have stagnated, and I've had to invest more just to keep occupancy up.

## Why the Pressure to Regulate Keeps Building

Growth has side effects for the people who live here year-round. Housing prices have gone up significantly as properties get converted to short-term rentals and as new rentals get built. More rentals also means more nuisance for neighbors.

Those two things seem to be putting political pressure on the county government from locals. I understand it. If you're a local watching home prices climb and traffic and noise increase, you want someone to act.

My concern is that when a government feels it needs to act, the result can be more than the problem calls for. Some of what's being proposed feels like that to me: the county wanting to take action, even though it could hurt the local economy more than necessary.

---

## The Proposal I'm Watching: Blueprints and Septic Inspections

The one I'm most focused on would require every rental to have blueprints drawn up and a septic inspection done for compliance. Hocking County is considering this. It's in the deliberative stage.

I split this into two parts.

**Septic inspections.** I think it's reasonable to expect a septic system to be adequate for the rental. A system that can't handle the guests is a real problem, and I've seen [what a septic failure mid-season can cost](/blog/septic-system-failure-lesson).

**Blueprints.** This is the part I think is an overreach. As proposed, there's no grandfathering. If you don't already have blueprints, you'd have to get them drawn up retroactively, which would cost hundreds of dollars per cabin.

This is a county that has had no building codes forever. Asking every owner to produce blueprints after the fact feels like an undue burden, especially for small owners.

I'd also point out that the market already polices unsafe or run-down places. Guests leave reviews saying so, and bad properties get driven off the market naturally.

Requiring blueprints for new builds or extensive additions makes more sense to me. That's where I hope this ends up.

I haven't done anything yet. I'm waiting to see how it shakes out. It doesn't make sense to spend time and money on something that is unknown at this point. There will also surely be a compliance period when the regs are finalized.

---

## Balance Matters Because the Local Economy Runs on Rentals

I'm not against regulation. Some rules to protect locals are fair, and I'd accept them.

But the area's industry is built on these rentals, and they support the local economy in many ways. I hire cleaners, maintenance people, HVAC techs, plumbers and electricians. Our guests also spend money in the area, which drives local commerce.

Rules that push small owners out don't only affect the owners. They affect everyone who gets work or business from them. That's why I keep coming back to balance: protect the locals, without hurting the economy more than it needs to be hurt.

## Join the Local Lodging Association

The best thing I can tell a host is to join the local short-term rental association, the [Hocking Hills Lodging Owners Association](https://www.hockinghillslodgingownersassociation.com/).

Here is what you get for $150 a year:

- Networking events with other owners
- Advocacy on behalf of owners
- Email updates on what's happening in the market

It works a bit like a union does for labor. Most owners are small and don't have the resources to advocate for themselves individually. The association is hiring lawyers, which is a good reason to be part of it and contribute, so owners can push back on some of the proposals.

A new or part-time host doesn't need to become an activist. Even being on the email list means you hear about changes before they affect you. I actually joined recently. I honestly didn't know we had an association until recently, and when I realized that regs were coming, it made me want to help support the advocacy group. It's also a good idea for me to start networking more in the area to get names of good contractors, etc.

## What I'd Do Before Buying in a Growing Market

If you're thinking about buying a cabin in a market that's growing quickly, here's what I'd do.

1. **Look for a lodging owners association.** It's the easiest way to keep up with local regulations, meet other owners and get updates by email.
2. **Do your research before you jump in.** Know what the local rules are today and how the market has been moving.
3. **If there's no association, don't panic.** In my view, that likely means it's too early to worry much about regulation. Research the market anyway.

Also keep in mind what I said about rates and occupancy. Competition has made things harder for me, so I wouldn't count on easy growth. My [rural cabin pricing guide](/blog/rural-cabin-pricing-guide) covers how I think about rates.

---

## Stay Informed and Stay Involved

A growing, lightly regulated market is a great place to be. But as a market gets more popular, the pressure for regulation only increases.

You can't stop that, but you can pay attention. Join the owners' association, read the proposals that affect you, and speak up when a rule goes further than it should. I'd like to see reasonable rules, like septic adequacy, and I'd like to see blueprints reserved for new builds and extensive additions.

Hocking Hills short-term rental regulations will keep changing. The hosts who stay informed and involved will be in a much better position than the ones who find out after the vote.
