Guests drive a long way to reach a rural cabin, and the last stretch is often where the cell signal gets weak or disappears. If their directions, door code and your phone number are all sitting in an app that won't load, a good arrival turns into a stressful one.

My cabins in Hocking Hills usually have enough service to at least send a text. Even so, guests have had to drive back toward a signal to reach me or to find their own check-in info. It rarely happens anymore, because each time it did, I added something to prevent it.

This post covers what I send and when, how I handle the harder-to-find cabin, and why the wifi password is my last line of defense when every reminder fails.

---

## Why Check-In Falls Apart When There's No Signal

The problem usually isn't that the information doesn't exist. It's that the guest has it in the wrong place. The door code is in the booking app, the directions are in a live map, and neither works once the bars drop.

So my whole approach is to get the guest to **download and save the important stuff before they leave home**. That means driving directions, access instructions and wifi details, all stored where they can open them without a connection.

I can't make a guest do that. I can make sure they know they need to, and make it easy.

---

## What Actually Happens When a Guest Can't Get Online

In my experience it's mild. A guest realizes they don't have the info, drives back until they get service, and texts me or looks it up. They tell me what happened, and they're usually not upset.

I still treat each time as a flaw in my process. After every instance, I add a reminder or some other step so the next guest doesn't have to make that drive. Over the years that's why it has become rare.

If you're newer to this, expect it to happen at least once. How you respond matters more than the fact that it happened. If a guest does get frustrated, [when a guest is looking for a reason to complain](/blog/guest-looking-to-complain) covers how I think about that.

## My Message Timeline From Booking to Arrival

I keep this simple. There are three touches:

1. **After booking:** a short message telling the guest to look for their stay info a few days before arrival.
2. **A few days before check-in:** once Airbnb, VRBO or whichever platform the guest booked through gives access to the arrival info, I send a message with everything they need and where to find it in their reservation.
3. **Same time, by text:** I also send a text message with the info. This is the one that matters for a no-service cabin, because a text is already on their phone. They can open it on the road without loading anything.

The first message sets the expectation, the second delivers the info, and the text puts a copy where it's easiest to reach.

## Directions for a Cabin That's Hard to Find

Most of my cabins are fine. For most guests, GPS gets them there without any trouble, and I tell them so. I don't want to send a wall of warnings about a route that works.

One of my cabins is trickier. Cell service there is very spotty, so I send much more detail for that one. I include **screenshots with notes and landmarks** so guests can follow the route even if the map stops updating. The instructions spell out the pitfalls to avoid.

Where guests go wrong is usually simple: they don't read the directions ahead of time and just put the address into GPS. I can't do much about it if they don't heed the advice.

The rule I follow is to match the amount of instruction to the difficulty of the trip. An easy cabin gets a simple "GPS works fine." A hard one gets screenshots and landmarks.

## Why the Wifi Password Is the Backup That Matters

Say the usual reminders failed. The guest never downloaded anything and has no cell data. The thing that saves them is **wifi**.

If they can get on the cabin's wifi, they're back online. From there they can look up the door code themselves, even if they didn't think to download it ahead of time.

That's why I make sure guests have the wifi info in **multiple places**: in the app, in my messages, and in a text message. As long as the text goes through before their stay, it's already on their phone, and they don't need a signal to open it. It doesn't depend on a map loading or an app working at the right moment.

I don't post the wifi info on the outside of the cabin. I password protect my wifi, and I'd rather not put that password where anyone can read it.

If your door code is the one thing standing between a guest and the cabin, also read [what to do when a smart lock fails](/blog/smart-lock-failure-backup-access). It's the same idea: always have a second way in.

## When Guests Still Get It Wrong

Even with all of this, it still happens occasionally, especially at the cabin that's hard to reach. People miss a turn or never open my messages.

I'll be honest: at some point I stop blaming myself. I send detailed instructions, a pre-arrival text and a heads-up in the app. I can't control whether someone reads what I send or reaches out when they're confused. I do all I can to get them there without stress, and I leave it at that.

That doesn't mean ignoring the problem. When a guest does get stuck, I take it as a chance to improve the process. But it's a guest-communication problem, not a sign that the system is broken. Setting expectations early also helps with reviews. [Handling bad reviews](/blog/handling-bad-reviews) goes deeper on that.

## What I'd Tell a New Host to Do First

If you're starting out, build the three-message timeline and the wifi backup before your first guest. Then use this rule: **send more reminders than you think you need, and cut back if it starts to feel overbearing.**

It's easier to remove a reminder than to fix a bad arrival. If guests tell you it's too much, trim it. If nobody complains, leave it alone.

Treat every no-service incident as feedback. Add one thing each time. Mine have shrunk to almost nothing that way.

Missing signal is also one of the [things guests expect at a rural cabin that city hosts never think about](/blog/rural-cabin-guest-expectations). Guests who know to plan for it are much easier to host.

---

## The Short Version

For check-in at a cabin with no cell service, tell guests early, send the info by text a few days out, and give them screenshots and landmarks if the route is tricky. Put the wifi info in more than one place, including that text, so they can reach the door code even if everything else fails.

You won't catch every guest. But with a clear timeline and a backup, almost none of them will be stuck at the end of a long drive.
