Hot Tub Maintenance for Rentals: The Spare Parts That Save Your Turnover Day

By Greg Myers
← Back to Blog

A hot tub is one of the highest-value amenities you can offer at a rural cabin — and one of the highest-maintenance. It's mechanical, it's exposed to constant use from guests who don't treat it the way they'd treat their own equipment at home, and when it breaks, it tends to break at the worst possible time.

I had exactly that experience recently: a hot tub sprang a leak right in the middle of a same-day turnover. Here's what happened, what I've learned about keeping spare parts on hand, and what I wish I'd known before buying the tubs I have now.

The Leak That Could Have Ruined a Turnover

The leak turned out to be the circulation pump — the component responsible for keeping water moving through the filtration and heating system. On a same-day turnover, there's no slack in the schedule to deal with an unexpected failure like that. Guests are arriving, housekeeping is on a clock, and a broken hot tub either gets fixed fast or it becomes a problem you're managing in real time with an incoming guest.

Luckily, I had a spare circulation pump on hand. I was able to swap it out without much stress at all, and the turnover proceeded on schedule. The old pump was about seven years old, which is roughly the point where these components start reaching the end of their expected life anyway, so this wasn't entirely a surprise — just poorly timed, the way these things usually are.

Why a Spare Circulation Pump Is Worth Keeping on Hand

The circulation pump is the one component I'd consider genuinely critical to keep a spare of. Without it, the tub isn't usable at all — no filtration, no heating cycle, nothing. If it fails and you don't have a replacement ready, you're looking at either an unusable hot tub for however long it takes to source and install a new one, or a guest complaint that's hard to avoid.

Circulation pumps are also fairly interchangeable across similar tub models, which makes keeping a spare a practical, low-hassle investment rather than something that requires tub-specific sourcing every time. If you've got a tub that's getting up toward that six or seven-year mark, having a spare pump already on the shelf means a failure becomes a quick swap instead of an emergency.

Blower Pumps Are a Lower Priority

Not every component deserves the same level of backup planning. Blower pumps — the ones responsible for the air jets — are a lower priority than the circulation pump. If a blower pump fails, the tub is still usable; guests just won't have the air jet function working. It's a downgrade in the experience, not a dealbreaker, and it usually doesn't generate complaints the way a completely nonfunctional tub would.

Because of that, I don't feel the same urgency to keep a blower pump in reserve. If one fails, you can order a replacement and swap it in on a normal timeline rather than treating it as an emergency.

Keep Spare O-Rings and Small Parts Too

Beyond the pumps themselves, it's worth keeping a small stock of spare O-rings and similar small hardware. These wear out more often than people expect, especially given how many chemicals are cycling through hot tub water on a regular basis. Chlorine, bromine, pH adjusters — all of that chemical exposure takes a toll on rubber seals and gaskets over time, and a worn O-ring can cause a leak just as disruptive as a failed pump, just usually smaller and slower to notice.

Keeping a small parts kit on hand — O-rings, basic seals, the kind of things that are cheap and easy to store — means a lot of minor hot tub issues become a five-minute fix instead of a service call.

What I've Learned About Buying Hot Tubs for Rentals

If you're in the market for a hot tub for a rental property, my advice, learned the hard way, is to keep it simple. Fewer jets, fewer features like head pillows, less overall complexity. It's tempting to buy the tub with the most bells and whistles, but in a rental setting, more complexity just means more things that can eventually break — and guests genuinely don't notice or care about the difference between a tub with forty jets and one with twenty. They're happy to have a hot tub at all. It checks the box on your listing regardless of how feature-loaded it is.

A simpler tub means fewer components to maintain, fewer things that can fail on a turnover day, and less money spent long-term on repairs and replacement parts for features that weren't adding much to the guest experience in the first place.

There's a second piece of buying advice that's just as important: once you find a brand and model line that works well, stick with it across your properties. Standardizing means the pumps, filters, and other components become interchangeable across multiple tubs, not just within one. That opens up the option to keep a genuinely comprehensive set of spares — one of everything — because those spares can cover any of your properties rather than being locked to a single tub. It also means less time spent researching parts for a one-off model every time something needs replacing.

Finding a Technician in a Rural Market

This is one of the more frustrating realities of running rental hot tubs in a rural area: qualified technicians are genuinely hard to find. In my market, Hocking Hills, there are only a couple of technicians serving an area with thousands of hot tubs between short-term rentals and private homes. That's a significant mismatch between demand and available service capacity.

Networking with other hosts is usually the most reliable way to find someone good — word of mouth tends to surface the technicians who are actually responsive and competent, which isn't always obvious from a search result. A general search is a reasonable fallback if you're in a pinch and don't have a network to draw on yet, but treat whoever you find well once you find them. When there are only a couple of providers in an entire region, you're somewhat at their mercy, and being a reliable, easy-to-work-with customer matters more than it might in a market with more competition.

There are also technicians based farther away, in my case out of the Columbus area, who will travel to rural properties. That's a real option, but expect them to book out further in advance and charge more for the travel and demand.

I'm honestly not sure why this market is so underserved relative to the number of hot tubs out there, but it's real, and it's pushed me toward doing more of the repair work myself. I've gotten decent at it over time, and I handle as many repairs as I can personally when I'm available — I'm even considering getting a formal certification so I can take on more of it directly rather than depending entirely on a thin local technician pool.

The Bottom Line

A hot tub failure doesn't have to become a crisis. Keep a spare circulation pump on hand once your tub is getting up in years, don't stress as much about blower pumps, stock a few spare O-rings and small parts, and when you're buying new equipment, resist the urge to over-invest in complexity that guests won't notice anyway. A little preparation turns what could be a stressful, guest-facing emergency into a routine swap that nobody but you ever has to think about.
