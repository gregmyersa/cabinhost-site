Seller Financing a Cabin: How I Used It to Grow

October 2026 · By Greg Myers
← Back to Blog

One of my best cabin deals didn't come from a bank. It came from a seller who agreed to get paid over time. I bought 42 secluded, wooded acres in Hocking Hills with a cabin that needed work, and I put down $25k.

Seller financing a cabin isn't easy to find, and it isn't right for every property. I sent hundreds of emails to find one seller who was interested, and then the deal took months to come together. It was worth it.

In this post I'll cover the terms I got, how I found the seller, why they said yes, and what it cost me to build the property out. I'll also cover the downsides, because there are real ones. I'm not an attorney, financial planner or CPA, so treat this as what I did and not as advice.

What My Seller-Financed Cabin Deal Looked Like

I closed on this deal in 2019. Here are the terms:

  • Purchase price: $240k
  • Down payment: $25k
  • Interest: 0% for 10 years
  • Payment: $800 a month
  • First payment: due at the beginning of year 4 after closing
  • Balloon payment: $120k at the end of the term

The payments started at the beginning of year 4. The clock didn't start until then.

The $25k covered closing costs and the agent commissions, and it left a little in the seller's pocket. I was a licensed agent at the time, so I waived my own commission and paid my brokerage separately. You can do this kind of deal without a license. I don't have one now, and I fully intend to buy more property this way.

What I got was a property with a rehabbable cabin and several building spots I could use later. I got it with very little cash, and the payments were low once it started renting. My goal was to rehab it and start earning on it with little money in, and to have good cash flow after it started renting.

How I Found a Seller Who Would Carry the Note

I'd been learning creative finance techniques, including seller-held financing. These deals need special circumstances, so I went looking for listings that looked like they could work.

The email I sent to listing agents was simple. I asked if the owner would be interested in getting paid over time. I said they would still get paid, and that I could put down enough to cover closing costs so the seller wouldn't have anything out of pocket to close. If they were stuck on their asking price, I said I could meet it. I included some sample terms but kept the email open-ended.

It took hundreds of emails to find one seller who was interested. Then it took months for that deal to come together. If you try this, expect a lot of silence and a lot of "no." You're looking for the one seller whose situation fits.

Why This Seller Said Yes

I got good terms because I got them their price.

The property had been listed for over a year. The owners wanted too much for a normal, arm's-length sale. But I was willing to pay their price if the terms worked for me, and they were willing to take the terms to get their number.

A few other things made this seller a good fit:

  • The listing agent was their daughter.
  • They'd owned the property for 20+ years and had no debt on it.
  • They were retired teachers and didn't need a lump sum.

People get a number in their head and stick to it, even when taking less up front would make more sense. I don't know why they wanted that price so badly, but I could work with it.

I also figured out the $800 payment by listening. In conversation I learned they had been getting $800 in rent on the property. That told me they'd be comfortable with $800 a month from me, with nothing to do except collect it in their bank account.

There may also be a tax angle for the seller. With second homes, spreading the sale over time can spread the capital gain across several years of income instead of one big year. That's my understanding, not tax advice. Sellers should check with their own CPA.

What I Put Into the Property After Closing

The cheap purchase was only the start. Here's what I spent afterward:

  • About $30k to rehab the first small cabin.
  • About $10k to replace the septic leach field. The old one lasted about 5 years after I bought. If you want to see how bad a septic problem can get, I wrote about what happens when your septic system fails mid-season.
  • About $180,000 to build and furnish a second cabin, a 4-bedroom log cabin.

I paid for the rehab and the new cabin with a line of credit. For the second cabin, I used other debt to tie into an old logging road that led to a flat spot that wasn't too wooded. I cleared some trees there and had a second great building site. It all cash flows very nicely.

It wasn't smooth. My biggest headache was the contractor. He didn't manage his money well, and I got left in the lurch a bit. He also had health issues toward the end of the project. It wasn't fun, but I got it done.

There was also some local politics. My contractor didn't want to use a local guy I knew for the septic and foundation work, because he wouldn't do the gravel the way the contractor wanted. That man was also a township trustee, and he was sore about losing the excavation job. He held up construction by saying we needed a driveway permit, even though we had tied into the existing drive. The other trustees got us back on track. In a small rural township, who you hire can matter more than you'd expect.

On top of that, I was getting married that same year at another property in the area. That property was hit by a tornado. I had to manage a salvage cut and cancel and refund all of that property's summer bookings. It was a stressful year.

The Real Risks of Seller Financing a Cabin

This structure has downsides, and a new host should know them before trying it.

Your equity is locked up. I can't use the equity in this property. A bank would want to be in first position, and I couldn't get the owners to agree to that. The property is probably worth about $800k now, and I can't cash anything out until the balloon. With interest rates where they are, I'm fine taking it slow.

The balloon is real. A balloon payment is a big bill at the end. I'm confident I can refinance when it comes due, but that depends on the property holding its value. By my estimate, I'd walk away with roughly $350k to $400k after paying off the balloon, assuming it doesn't appreciate any more. That's an estimate.

The seller has to trust you. This is the biggest obstacle. The seller has to believe they'll actually get paid month after month. So I put their payment first. It's sent automatically into their bank account early each month.

The paperwork matters. I used a title company or attorney, and I'd make sure they're well versed in seller financing. I wrote the terms right into the purchase contract, and the note and mortgage were drawn up from those terms. I know how to do this, but if you don't, hire a realtor or attorney who can draw up the contract. Check with your attorney and your CPA before you sign.

The location has trade-offs. The property is off the beaten path and further from the park than other rentals. That's a small price for a deal this good, but I'll cover below how it's started to show.

When Revenue Slips: What I'm Changing This Fall

Revenue at this property has been down lately. There's growing competition from rentals in better locations, and being further from the park is a disadvantage. It's still profitable and a great deal. But I'm not going to sit and wait.

This fall I'm focusing on the log cabin, since it's my most productive one there:

  • Adding a sauna (I wrote about adding saunas to stand out in a crowded market)
  • Changing the beds so more people fit
  • Adding dressers
  • Adding a new firepit and a playground
  • Replacing the couch

The smaller cabin isn't getting a lot of bookings. The cabins are still booking fine, but revenue is just down about 15% from the peak. I just have to lower the price. If you're weighing the same call, my guide to pricing a rural cabin covers how I think about it. On the smaller cabin I'm adding a better bedframe, redoing the fire pit and replacing the couch. Sometimes one property slows down. If yours does, my post on why a Hocking Hills cabin isn't getting bookings covers where I'd look first. These upgrades should bring revenue back up a bit.

Would I Do a Seller-Financed Deal Again?

Yes. Right now it's tough to get into a deal like this because people are valuing their rentals too high. But I think when the market takes a significant dip, there will be more opportunities. I'll be ready to look without putting a lot of capital into each deal.

I closed in 2019, and I still have years of 0% payments left. By the time the balloon comes due, I expect the property to be worth well more than the $120k balloon, and I expect to refinance easily.

If you're a host with limited cash and a property you'd like to buy, don't rule out seller financing a cabin. Expect to send a lot of emails, and be willing to pay the seller's price in return for terms that work for you. Get the paperwork done properly, and remember that you'll still need cash to rehab and build.
