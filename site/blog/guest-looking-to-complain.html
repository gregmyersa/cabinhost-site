When a Guest Is Looking for a Reason to Complain

By Greg Myers
← Back to Blog

Most guest complaints are legitimate. Something broke, something wasn't clean, something didn't match the listing. You fix it, you make it right, everyone moves on. That's just hosting.

But every host eventually runs into a different kind of guest — one who isn't looking for a resolution. They're looking for leverage. They know a single one-star review can do real damage to a listing's ranking and take a dozen five-star reviews to fully offset, and some guests have figured out how to use that as a lever to extract refunds, upgrades, or concessions that have nothing to do with what actually happened during their stay.

It's one of the more frustrating situations in hosting, because the review system that's supposed to protect guests from bad hosts can just as easily be used to hold a good host hostage.

How to Recognize It

The tell isn't any single complaint — it's the pattern.

Real issues tend to be specific, proportionate, and singular: the hot tub wasn't heated, a light was out, the WiFi went down. A guest with a genuine problem tells you about it clearly and is generally satisfied once it's addressed.

The guests fishing for something different usually show a different pattern. Complaints that don't quite match reality — something described as filthy that was, by any reasonable measure, clean. Complaints that stay vague or keep shifting, so that fixing one thing just surfaces a new grievance instead of resolving the conversation. And often, complaints that start suspiciously early — sometimes before they've even finished settling in, as if they're building a case rather than reporting an experience.

None of these signs alone is proof. Sometimes a guest really did have a rough string of bad luck. But when you see the pattern stacking up — vague, shifting, disproportionate, early — it's worth adjusting your approach.

My Approach, Step by Step

I handle these situations guest by guest, because context matters. But there's a general sequence I follow.

Start by actually trying to fix the problem. If something is legitimately broken — a fridge, an appliance, anything mechanical — I address it as quickly as I can, the same as I would for any guest. This step isn't about the guest's intentions. It's about doing right by whoever is staying at the property, and it also establishes, for my own records and for anyone reading later, that I responded promptly and in good faith.

If the complaints are about something minor, like cleaning, I offer a partial refund of the cleaning fee. This resolves most situations. Most guests, even ones who are genuinely annoyed, are satisfied by a reasonable gesture that acknowledges their frustration.

If they keep pushing after that, I'll refund the rest of the cleaning fee. At this point, it's usually becoming clear whether this is a guest who will be reasonable or one who won't. A guest who's satisfied stops here. A guest who keeps escalating past a full cleaning fee refund over what started as a minor issue is telling you something about what they're actually after.

If it's still not enough, I lean fully into "the customer is always right." I apologize sincerely, make sure they feel heard and important, and treat their concerns with genuine care regardless of what I privately think is going on. This isn't about winning an argument with the guest — I'm not going to win that argument, and trying to isn't productive.

If it's clear a bad review might be coming anyway, I'll offer one more small gesture — a modest refund for their trouble. This might seem like giving in, but it's actually the most important step in the whole sequence, and it's not really for the guest at all.

Why That Last Step Matters

Here's the part that took me a while to fully appreciate: your response to a review, and the record of how you handled the situation, is written for future guests, not for the person who left it.

If a guest leaves a one-star review after all of this, I'm in a strong position to respond. I can say, honestly, that they received a generous refund for their trouble, that we did everything we could to resolve their concerns as they came up, and that we always want our guests to feel taken care of. That response, sitting right next to a one-star review, does a lot of work. A future guest reading it sees a host who was responsive, generous, and fair — even in a review that's technically negative. In some ways, a bad review handled this way can end up building more trust than it costs.

The alternative — refusing to engage, getting defensive, or holding firm on principle — leaves you with a one-star review and no good answer for it. The math on that isn't close. A modest refund is cheap. A poorly-handled bad review, sitting unaddressed on your listing indefinitely, costs a lot more over time.

How You Word It Matters

Every message you send during one of these interactions is potentially something a future guest, or even a platform mediator, will read later. That changes how you should write them.

Keep messages factual, warm, and specific. Document what the guest reported, what you did in response, and when. "I'm sorry to hear the fridge wasn't working — I've arranged for a repair technician to come out this afternoon, and in the meantime here's a nearby store for anything you need kept cold" reads completely differently, to anyone reading it later, than a vague "sorry for the trouble." The first shows a host actively solving a problem. The second shows nothing.

Avoid anything that sounds defensive or dismissive, even if you're frustrated. Comments like "that's not accurate" or "the listing clearly states..." might feel justified in the moment, but they read poorly out of context, and they give the guest ammunition to escalate. You can hold your ground on facts without sounding combative — stick to what you did and what you're offering, not what you think of their complaint.

And keep a paper trail. Refunds, apologies, offers — all of it should exist in writing through the platform's own messaging system, not just a phone call. If it ever comes down to a review dispute or a platform mediation, that record is what protects you.

Why This Math Works Out

It can feel like giving in every time you issue another small refund. It's worth actually running the numbers, because the math tells a different story.

A single one-star review can meaningfully hurt your search ranking and your overall rating for a long stretch of time. If your listing sits at a strong average — say, 4.9 stars across 60 reviews — one new one-star review drags that down more than most hosts expect, and clawing it back typically takes multiple five-star reviews in a row just to return to where you started. That's not a one-time cost. It's a cost that compounds every day your ranking is suppressed and fewer people see your listing at all.

Compare that to the actual dollar cost of a cleaning fee refund, or even a partial refund on the stay itself. In almost every case, the total amount you'd refund to fully appease a difficult guest is a small fraction of what a suppressed ranking costs you over the following weeks and months in lost bookings. It's not close. The refund feels like the bigger loss in the moment because it's immediate and specific. The ranking damage is the bigger loss in reality — it's just spread out and harder to see.

What It Means for Future Bookings

Once you've been through this with a guest, it's worth thinking about whether you'd want them back.

In most cases, my answer is no — not out of spite, but because the pattern rarely improves on a second stay. If a guest showed you during their first visit that they escalate complaints regardless of what you offer, there's no reason to expect a different dynamic the second time. I'm not going to go out of my way to block a repeat booking if it happens, but I'm also not going to proactively invite that guest back the way I would with someone who had a great, low-friction stay.

This is also part of why the earlier steps matter so much. Handling the situation calmly and generously the first time doesn't just protect the review — it also means that if this guest does come back, or if any dispute needs to be revisited, your record shows a host who acted in good faith throughout.

The Bigger Problem: This Shouldn't Be On Hosts Alone

It's worth naming plainly: this dynamic exists because the power between hosts and guests on review-based platforms is deeply asymmetric. A guest can threaten a review that does real financial damage to a host's business, with almost no verification of the underlying claim and almost no consequence to the guest for making it. Hosts, meanwhile, have very limited tools to dispute an unfair review, even when there's a clear pattern of bad-faith behavior.

That's not a problem any individual host can solve through better communication skills alone. It's a structural issue with how these platforms have built their review systems, and it's something hosts as a group have a legitimate interest in pushing back on — through platform feedback channels, host communities, and collectively raising the issue when platforms solicit input. The tactics in this post are how you protect yourself within the system as it currently exists. They're not a substitute for the system itself needing to get fairer.

This Isn't About Being a Pushover

None of this means rolling over for every complaint or assuming every unhappy guest is acting in bad faith. Most complaints are real, and most guests are reasonable. This approach is specifically for the minority who aren't — and even with them, the goal isn't to "win." It's to make sure that whatever review results from the interaction, you have a clean, honest, well-documented response sitting next to it.

You're not trying to convince the guest of anything at that point. You're writing for the next person who's deciding whether to book.
