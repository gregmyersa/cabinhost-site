How to Photograph Your Cabin Like a Pro (Without Hiring One)

By Greg Myers  ·  CabinHost Consulting

Good photos are the single biggest lever you have on a listing's performance, and most hosts either overspend on them or underinvest entirely. You can hire a professional photographer — though it's genuinely harder to coordinate for a rural property than a city listing — but the truth is you don't need to. A recent phone with a good camera, done thoughtfully, gets you most of the way there.

Here's how I approach it at Red Oak Retreats.

Your Phone Is Good Enough — If It's the Right Phone

You don't need a DSLR. A recent iPhone, Pixel, or Samsung with a good wide-angle lens will produce photos that hold up perfectly well against professional shots, especially once guests are viewing them on a phone screen themselves, which is how most bookings happen anyway.

The wide-angle camera specifically matters. Cabin rooms tend to be smaller and more enclosed than the open-concept spaces phone cameras are often optimized for, and a wide lens lets you capture the whole room without backing into a wall.

Prep the Space Before You Shoot

Photos only look as good as the space you're photographing. Before you pick up your phone:

Declutter everything. Counters clear, personal items put away, nothing distracting in the frame. A clean, uncluttered space photographs dramatically better than the same space with clutter — even minor clutter most people wouldn't consciously notice while standing in the room.

Make the beds look great. This is one of the highest-impact details in the whole shoot. A perfectly made bed with fluffed pillows photographs like a magazine spread. A slightly rumpled one photographs like nobody cared.

Maximize the light. Open every blind and curtain. Turn on every light in the room. Shoot when the most natural light is coming into that particular space — that might be a different time of day for a west-facing bedroom versus an east-facing kitchen. Walk the property at different times if you need to and figure out when each room looks its best.

Golden hour for exteriors. For anything shot outside — the cabin exterior, the deck, the hot tub, the fire pit area — golden hour is the difference between a flat, ordinary photo and one that actually sells the experience. The warm light in early morning or evening does more for a rural property's photos than almost any other single factor.

Shooting Each Space Differently

Not every room in the cabin photographs the same way, and treating them identically is a missed opportunity.

The kitchen. Shoot from a corner to capture the most counter space and cabinetry in one frame. Clear the counters completely except for maybe one or two intentional touches — a bowl of fruit, a cutting board. Turn on the range hood light and any under-cabinet lighting in addition to the overheads; kitchens often have more light sources than people think to use.

Bathrooms. These are small spaces where a wide-angle lens does the most work. Close the toilet lid, remove any personal toiletries, fold towels neatly (or hang fresh ones), and make sure the mirror doesn't catch your reflection or the phone in the shot. Good bathroom photos reassure guests about cleanliness more than almost any other room.

Bedrooms. Shoot from the doorway or the corner opposite the bed to capture the whole room. This is where the "beds look great" rule matters most — hotel-cornered sheets, fluffed pillows, maybe a folded throw blanket at the foot of the bed. A well-styled bed can carry an entire bedroom photo even if the rest of the room is simple.

Outdoor amenities. Hot tubs, fire pits, decks, and views are often what actually sells a rural cabin, so give them real attention. Shoot the hot tub with the water visibly clear and ideally with steam rising if the temperature allows — that single detail communicates comfort instantly. Fire pit areas photograph best in the early evening, right as light is fading, sometimes with the fire lit. Wide shots that capture the whole outdoor living area help guests picture themselves there, not just see that the amenity exists.

Common areas. Living rooms and great rooms benefit from a slightly wider angle and a shot that shows how the space connects to the rest of the cabin — sightlines into the kitchen or out to a porch help guests understand the flow of the property, not just isolated boxes of rooms.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

A few things I see repeatedly on underperforming listings:

Inconsistent lighting across photos. A listing where half the photos are bright and warm and the other half are dim and blue-toned looks unprofessional and makes guests wonder what they're actually going to find. Shoot every room with the same intentionality.

Too few photos of the amenities that matter most. If your hot tub or fire pit is a major selling point, one photo of it isn't enough. Multiple angles, different times of day, and detail shots communicate value far better than a single wide shot buried on page two.

Vertical phone photos. Most platforms display listing photos in a horizontal format. Shooting vertically means your photo gets cropped or displayed awkwardly. Always shoot horizontal, even on a phone.

Photographing clutter you've stopped noticing. After living with or managing a property for a while, it's easy to stop seeing things a first-time visitor would immediately notice — a stray cord, a full trash can, mismatched furniture. Walk through with fresh eyes, or better yet, have someone else do a walkthrough before you shoot.

Not shooting or updating photos across seasons. A cabin surrounded by snow looks very different from the same cabin in full summer foliage. If your photos only reflect one season, you're not accurately representing the property for a large portion of the year. Get outdoor shots across all four seasons, and be selective about the day you shoot each one — for winter, you want enough snow on the ground to look pretty without shooting in the middle of a blizzard. Wait for a clear, calm day with fresh snow rather than settling for whatever day you happen to be free.

Once you have a full set of seasonal exterior shots, rotate your top photo and gallery order to match whichever season is currently booking. A listing showing golden fall foliage in October books differently than one still showing summer photos from six months ago. Guests are picturing their specific trip, and seeing the property in a setting close to what they'll actually experience builds confidence.

Letting interior photos go stale. It's not just the outside that needs updates. If you've changed bedding, added furniture, or updated any amenities since your last photo shoot, guests are going to arrive to something that doesn't match what they saw when booking. That mismatch, even when the change is an improvement, chips away at trust. Reshoot whenever something meaningfully changes inside the cabin, not just on some fixed annual schedule.

Choosing and Ordering Your Photos

Once you've got a strong set of photos, how you select and order them matters almost as much as how you shot them.

Your first photo is the most important decision you'll make. It's the thumbnail guests see in search results before they click anything else, and it needs to earn that click. It doesn't have to be the exterior — often it shouldn't be. Whatever image most powerfully captures the feeling of being at your property is the right choice, whether that's a glowing hot tub at dusk or a stunning view from the deck.

The next four photos matter almost as much. On most platforms, your first five photos are what show up on the listing page itself when a guest clicks in — not just the search thumbnail. That means those five images are doing the real work of converting a click into a booking. Don't default to room order for this set. Pull your strongest, most varied shots regardless of where they fall in the house, and make sure those five specifically stand out rather than just being the first five you happen to like.

Which photos belong in that top five depends on what your property's real selling points are, and that can shift with the season. If your outdoor space — a deck, a view, a fire pit area — is the standout feature, lead with it during spring, summer, and fall when guests are picturing time outside. If your strongest feature is something indoors, like a great game room or a cozy living space that doesn't depend on weather, it may make more sense to keep those photos in the top five year-round rather than swapping in outdoor shots that aren't your property's real strength. Know what's actually pulling guests toward your listing and build the top five around that, not around an assumption that outdoor photos always have to lead.

After that first five, work through the property logically: living spaces, kitchen, bedrooms, bathrooms, then remaining outdoor areas and amenities. A guest should be able to scroll through and mentally walk through the property in a way that makes sense, ending with anything that reinforces why they'd want to book — a great outdoor shot, a cozy evening scene, something aspirational to close on.

Cut anything mediocre. A weak photo doesn't add information guests need — it just dilutes the strength of the photos around it. It's better to have twenty-five strong photos than forty where fifteen are forgettable.

Keep File Sizes Reasonable

This is something a lot of hosts get wrong in the opposite direction — they shoot in the highest possible resolution their phone allows and upload massive files.

You don't need that. Shoot at a slightly lower resolution, or compress your photos before uploading. There are two real advantages to this:

First, your listing loads faster. A guest browsing on their phone, possibly on a mediocre connection, is going to bounce off a slow-loading listing before the photos even finish appearing. Speed matters more than most hosts realize, and it matters even more on rural search traffic where connections aren't always great.

Second, uploading is faster on your end too. Massive files take longer to upload one by one, and if you're managing multiple properties or updating photos seasonally, that adds up.

A screen — phone or computer — genuinely can't tell the difference between a photo shot at full resolution and one that's been reasonably compressed. The visual quality holds up. The only thing you lose is unnecessary file size.

Consider a 3D Walkthrough

If you want to go a step further than photos, a 3D walkthrough is one of the most valuable things you can add to a listing. I use Matterport, and it gives prospective guests a real sense of the space before they book — not just individual room photos, but how the whole property flows and feels.

The equipment doesn't need to be expensive. A basic kit that uses your cell phone runs a few hundred dollars, and you don't need the high-end camera versions — those are overkill for a vacation rental listing. My advice: check eBay for a lightly used kit. A lot of sellers are people who bought one, used it a handful of times, and are moving on. You can often find a great deal that way.

The one scenario where I'd be a bit more cautious is buying from someone who was a realtor liquidating their entire business — that's not the typical seller, and it's worth asking a few more questions in that case. But for the average lightly-used listing, it's a solid way to get professional-feeling equipment at a fraction of retail.

A walkthrough gives guests genuine insight into what they're actually booking, which builds trust before they arrive and reduces the chance of a mismatch between expectation and reality.

The Bottom Line

You don't need professional equipment or a professional photographer to get photos that convert. You need a decent phone, good light, a clean and staged space, and a little bit of technical awareness about file size. Add a 3D walkthrough if you want to stand out even further.

The hosts who get this right aren't spending more money — they're just being more thoughtful about the process.
